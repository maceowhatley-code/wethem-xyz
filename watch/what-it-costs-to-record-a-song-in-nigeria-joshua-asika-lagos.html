What it costs to record a song in Nigeria: Joshua Asika.

Joshua Asika · Reported by Maceo · Lagos · 17 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week →

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

Seventeen minutes in Lagos with Joshua Asika, a rapper from Taraba State, and the plainest account of creative economics anywhere in this archive. Maceo is in Nigeria on his first trip to Africa, deepening the partnership with Creative Alchemy, and Joshua is one of the earliest members of the community: somebody who had been turning up to Discord calls for two years before the two of them were ever in a room.

It opens on the country. “I think it’s a whole lot of wahala here.” Asked about the economy, he does not perform outrage about it. “I think the Nigerian system is sometimes hard to cope with. We Nigerians get used to adapt into every situation.” Asked whether people can change any of it, he has already run the experiment: there are protests over the exchange rate, the fuel, the gas, and “But it doesn’t work. It depends on the government.”

Then the numbers, which are the reason to watch. His first song, around 2014, was a collab called Success with two friends from the neighbourhood, “it’s called Success is the title of the song”, and the three of them split the studio cost because none of them could carry it alone. “The expenses is less for each and every one of us.” The session was 3,000 naira. Maceo works the July rate out loud, “1,500 Naira is is around like $1”, and stops to say that people do not understand how far two dollars goes in supporting a creative here. Today the same session is out of reach: “You can record for 20,000 right now.”

What pays for it is the detail nobody would invent. “I have a small piggery.” He sells the pigs, and will “use part of the money to record and buy feeds for the pigs”: a rap career funded by livestock, one animal at a time.

The hardship is not only the obstacle. It is the material. “Sometimes when things get harder you you get more inspiration. Like you have to speak your mind.”

He is precise about what his part of the country costs a musician. In the north, “the parents and and people around you will like discourage you”, and what they say is “you can’t succeed through music”. His own father is a pastor and did the opposite. “My dad have been supportive. My mom has been supportive.” What his father actually says is four words: “Boy, just keep going.” Asked what his family is like, he reaches for the highest thing available to him. “If I could come again in in another world I could choose my family.”

He is 24, second of four, and the one the money moves from. “The support comes from me to them.” He says the responsibility is what makes him work.

Asked whether he will leave, he catches the cliche and takes it apart. Everyone says leave your comfort zone, but leaving is not the question: “I’m about to leave my comfort zone, but what am I going to do?” The ambition itself is not local. “I’m not just singing for for Taraba State people, I’m singing for people globally.”

He looks up to Wizkid, first heard on the radio, “Holla at Your Boy”, then Davido, Burna Boy, Rema and Asake, for taking Afrobeats out of the country entirely.

The only advice he offers in seventeen minutes is addressed to everybody else rather than to himself: “keep pushing no matter where you’re coming from”. It is the last thing he says, and he has just spent a quarter of an hour explaining exactly what it costs him to take it.
