Buying abandoned houses to build a neighborhood.

Kiba Gateaux, Reported by Maceo, Online, 58 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

To quote this piece: Kiba Gateaux, “Buying abandoned houses to build a neighborhood,” We Them Media, https://wethem.xyz/watch/web3-pop-up-cities-and-akiya-houses-japan. Each transcript line links to its second.

Just under an hour of the podcast, recorded from Philadelphia with a guest sitting in rural Japan. The guest is Kiba Gateaux, who is buying abandoned houses in Nagano and calling the result Zoo City Japan, and the talk runs from real estate paperwork to Shintoism to whether Ethereum is doing what it said it would.

He gives the resume quickly. “my name is Kiba. I’ve been in crypto for about 9 years now”, he says: ConsenSys, then DeFi, then DAOs, and then he “got really tired of DAOs after many years of watching them not work”. What pushed him out was not a market. “I kind of saw crypto just like not being crypto anymore and not being punk or anarchist and not really caring about the values”, he says, so he went to Zuzalu instead.

The problem he kept finding there is ownership. In pop-up cities, network states and ordinary co-living alike, “no one actually like owns the land that they’re on or what they’re trying to do”, so somebody keeps paying the rent, and when that person leaves it ends. His answer was blunt: “hey guys, why don’t we just buy a bunch of houses?” The joke is on the friends who asked him to organise it. “And then nobody bought a house, which I think is really funny because now I’ve bought multiple houses and they’re like ridiculously cheap.”

He is not a resident. “I technically don’t live here. I don’t have a visa, but I have like 3 houses now”, he says, all within a five minute walk of each other. The number behind that: “There’s currently now the number floating around is 9 million abandoned houses in Japan”, about fifteen percent of all houses, and closer to twenty in Nagano. The causes he gives are depopulation, everyone leaving for Tokyo, and a nineteen eighties bubble in which the Imperial Palace, five or ten acres of land, “was worth more than all of the real estate in California combined”. Then inheritance tax, which is why “there’s actually like property that’s owned by dead people still because the title never kind of got transferred”.

On staying a tourist he is unsentimental. Japan, he says, needs immigrants: “they do absolutely fundamentally need immigrants because their population’s already dying”. Later, flatly: “I totally understand Japanese racism now, or xenophobia.” His own arrangement he puts this way: “I feel like I’m integrated into the society, but I’m not integrated into the system.”

The middle is the pop-up city itself. Zuzalu he treats as the only real one, and the format as the point: “that’s what a pop-up city is. It’s like an experiment in living.” Running his own twice, he took the light approach: “I feel like it went pretty well, mostly because I did the bare minimum”, because “why am I going to make my life hell for a bunch of other people for no reason?” He bid on a school the city could not sell, “it turns out nobody had bid on this school for 9 months”, and lost it anyway: “of course I didn’t get it because I’m sure it’s like local politics and someone sold it to their friend or whatever”.

The long plan he calls Brooklyn 2.0. “my thesis is that like Japanese culture and like American Black culture are the 2 strongest cultures in the whole world”, he says, noting that “never in my life have I really ever seen like Black people try to be Japanese”.

Asked at the end where Ethereum is, he grants it the world computer and withholds the rest. “we’re gonna like listen to the regulators and do lobbying instead of just being punk”, he says of the space, and “I think we’re losing the fight”. His question for the world is four words long, and he calls it basic.
