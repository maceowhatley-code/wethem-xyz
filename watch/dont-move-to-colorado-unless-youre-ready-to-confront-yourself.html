Tim at Cheesman Park: a lore walk through old Denver money.

Reported by Maceo, Denver, 6 min · filmed 19 March 2026

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

A night walk through a Denver park with a group who know its history, which turns into the plainest advice on this channel about moving to Colorado: do not do it unless you are ready to meet yourself.

The lore comes first, delivered by pointing. An open field where, they say, Amelia Earhart landed on her first tour of the country, the rich in the pavilion and the poor on the far side. A gravedigger paid a dollar seventy a day. And the reason nobody wanted the bodies moved. The verdict on the place is unanimous: “No other park in Denver has the energy that people bring to this park.”

One of them explains what that energy is. “People come here with their personal problems, and they try to solve them. Come here on a Saturday, a Sunday during the summer, you’ll probably never want to leave Denver, because you probably met five people that you’ve been waiting to meet your whole life.” He is honest that night is different, and that he does not usually come after dark, and that something pulls him here anyway.

Then the speech. He moved to Capitol Hill after a breakup, having lived in Winter Park in 2015 and fallen for the state: “Only place I ever felt home, for real.” He tried New York, Washington, Florida, and came back to find he had picked up where he left off. But he does not want anyone to follow him without hearing the rest.

“A lot of people hate it. You’re going to find some very difficult, lonely times. You’re going to be battling with your own mind. Who are you as a person? If you’re not ready to confront yourself, don’t move to Colorado.” The people you meet in the first month, he warns, are mostly leaving next week. “Unless you want to follow them, and guess what, you’re going to ruin your life anyway. Be yourself. Be proud of yourself. Know you’re born with a gift.”

His own summary of two and a half years: “It really puts you in the position of, are you going to own up to your stuff or not? And if not, promise you, you’re not going to be here.”

It closes with something else entirely. One of the group asks for a moment to thank the Denver Communists, because they were the organisation that showed up when his cousin, Paul Castaway, was killed in Denver in July 2015. “He was my guy.” That is where the walk ends.

Language is unfiltered; the transcript is bleeped where the captions were.
