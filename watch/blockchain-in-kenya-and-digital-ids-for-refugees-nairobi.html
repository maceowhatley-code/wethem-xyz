A developer building digital IDs for people without papers.

James Wasonga, Nairobi, 23 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

To quote this piece: James Wasonga, “A developer building digital IDs for people without papers,” We Them Media, https://wethem.xyz/watch/blockchain-in-kenya-and-digital-ids-for-refugees-nairobi. Each transcript line links to its second.

Twenty-three minutes in Nairobi, filmed at a hackathon called Hack the Dot, with a Kenyan developer building identity software. The batteries almost ran out before it started, and the first question is the simple one: how do people in Kenya actually see this technology.

James Wasonga answers it in two halves. There is the technology, which he describes as “a revolutionary technology meaning the upcoming technology whereby people are diving into blockchain as a whole”, and then there is the way most people actually arrive at it, which is not through the technology at all. “People see it as a money like I want to have some Ethereum or can I be a trader and I get some Bitcoin?”

He is bullish in a way the page will not editorialise about: he thinks adoption raises a country, “any country that employs economy employs Bitcoin means the economy automatically gets to shoot up”, and he sets that against what Kenya’s economy actually runs on. “the gross domestic product in Kenya like it’s mainly from the agricultural sector”. The main export is tea. So the useful question, and he gets to it himself, is what the technology does for the sector that already exists: “several applications have been developed to enable farmers to be able to get paid in Bitcoin”, and a payment between two people that never touches fiat, “we just send Bitcoin and you get transacted directly”.

Asked about the region, he goes somewhere unexpected. He says he has covered almost all of East Africa and has spent the last four or five months in Ethiopia, and what he wants to say about it is about movement and peace rather than markets. “In East Africa you move without any border”. And: “So East Africa we say no war, no violence.”

The centre of the tape is his project. “I’m building an application in my country, I mean, in sub-Saharan Africa, and I do like to generate digital IDs.” The problem he is aiming at is documentation: refugee and displaced populations who cannot prove who they are, and therefore cannot get anything from an institution, because “you must have a valid identification for that specific country”. His design takes biometrics and whatever document a person carries from the country they came from, and out of it, “The certificate itself will be able to generate a digital ID for him. A unique ID”. He does not want to run it alone: “I’m planning to integrate the application with government websites and government sites through partnership.” It is not meant to stop at the hackathon either. “Not totally a prototype mode but a live mode.”

Two definitions close the piece out, both asked plainly and answered plainly. A blockchain: “let me just say it’s a decentralized secure and transparent web 2 itself. So, it’s an improved web 2.” A meme coin: the easiest coin for a developer to make, “a coin with the least lowest value”, where “within 0.1 seconds its value can drop to zero you lose everything”, most of them built on Solana, “the mother of meme coins”.

The last stretch is mostly the interviewer, on creators, extraction and what infrastructure artists do not have. Wasonga’s own sign-off is shorter. “Make it simple like begin and make it simple and stupid”, and then, twice: “just keep building”.
