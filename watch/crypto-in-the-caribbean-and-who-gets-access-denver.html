Access, the Caribbean, and a conference at home.

Stefen Delevaux, Reported by Maceo, Denver, 11 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

To quote this piece: Stefen Delevaux, “Access, the Caribbean, and a conference at home,” We Them Media, https://wethem.xyz/watch/crypto-in-the-caribbean-and-who-gets-access-denver. Each transcript line links to its second.

Eleven minutes at ETH Denver 2024 with Stefen Delevaux, who runs Oasis Onchain, lives in the Bahamas, and had put on his own conference there the month before this was filmed. The conversation is about access: who has it, who does not, and what a blockchain is actually for.

The week itself he describes as busy in the usual way. “there’s so much going on in this place so many people to talk to”, he says, and he spent barely any time in the main venue. He came mainly to help friends put on Guild Day, for a company he describes as community focused and originally about DAO tools.

The route he took into this is the useful part of the tape. His background is economics: “my background is in economics. That’s what I studied in school”, he says, and he fell into finance without being suited to it, since “I was never really a finance person”. Fintech caught him first, because the story it told was that it was accessible and different from traditional banking. He followed it for a while and concluded “the narrative wasn’t quite true”. It was, he says, “of course completely connected to the traditional Financial system and it wasn’t accessible especially not in the Caribbean and the global South in general”, and he gives the flat example: “even if you think of like venmo and cash app right we have no access to it”.

Jaded with that, he read up on how blockchains actually work, and “that’s when the light bulb went off”. What he saw was “something that is a different system and it is globally accessible”. He then found his way to DAOs, and to a connection back to his own undergraduate thesis, which was on cooperatives. “it always comes back to community it always comes back to like worker ownership and Collective ownership”, he says. The through line he names himself: “the goal for me is just always empowerment”.

Asked for two ways this shows up in practice, he does not talk about price. It is access to payments, access to transactions, and “it’s access to a system that no one can shut you out of”, which is why he keeps returning to education and to simply showing people what crypto is.

Asked what impact he wants, the answer is local rather than grand. “I’m from the Bahamas I live in the Bahamas”, he says, and making life easier for the average person there, or in the Caribbean generally, would satisfy him. What that means concretely is “access to Banking and Finance in general”, taken for granted elsewhere, along with the ordinary business of cross border payments and wires and “being able to send something to you know a family member or like on a different Island for example”. Is that a daily problem, he is asked. “every day every day every day for sure”. Beyond inefficiency, he says, there is “complete lack of access in a lot of ways”, and being partly blocked from transacting with the rest of the world means you cannot work to the best of your ability or get paid for what you are good at. “it’s very basic but it’s very fundamental”.

His core memory after roughly seven years in the space is not a trade. It is the conference. Oasis On Chain was “the hardest thing I’ve ever done” and “it was incredibly difficult to just make happen”, and it worked: “this is awesome and this is this was worth it and it had impact which is like that’s everything”.
