The last day of ETH Safari in Kilifi, streamed live.

The last day of ETH Safari 2025 in Kilifi, Kenya, streamed live, opens with a game about how people trade when nobody has cash. Twenty people get cards and try to collect seven of one color, first by direct barter, then by swapping through shared piles of cards. By the leader’s count, the room made 32 in the barter round and over 100 with the pools, in the same amount of time. The game’s leader calls the second round “commitment pooling” and compares the piles to the pools on Uniswap. Without money, the leader says, “we do what our ancestors always did across all of Africa”: keep common pools of resources and keep track of who took what. On a blockchain, the chain “does the accounting.”

Then come the short talks. A speaker from Scroll explains what the network is and how builders can join its community, and Web3 Clubs describes its cohorts for early-stage founders. A talk on DePIN, decentralized physical infrastructure, covers weather stations, do-it-yourself GPS stations with cases 3D printed in Nairobi, and a smart meter for solar power. The first one in Kenya is on the speaker’s parents’ solar system, and they now earn “carbon micro credits.”

Taylor Lahey, a brand designer, wrote a children’s book about exchange. In the story Taylor tells, the city of Curitiba, Brazil, once traded bus tokens for litter. Over time, shops “started accepting them for goods.”

A security panel takes on keeping users and their assets safe: what audits cost, bug bounties, open source tools, the npm supply chain attack, social engineering and zero-knowledge proofs. One panelist’s number one advice for new developers: “join a community.”

The last panel of ETH Safari 2025 is about community itself. The panelists, from Kenya and beyond, talk about measuring a community and the distance from the people they serve. They talk about handing leadership on, and why most of their groups run on WhatsApp. One puts it simply: “go where the community is.” The stream closes with a pop quiz on stage, then chairs pulled into a circle for feedback.
