Maceo in the guest chair, telling the origin story.

Maceo, Online, 56 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

Fifty five minutes on somebody else’s podcast, with Maceo in the guest chair. It is season three, episode two of Let’s Talk about Web 3, a livestream with a chat running, co-hosted by Ohnahji, and the hosts spend it asking Maceo how We Them Media happened.

The way in was a college roommate trading NFT profile pictures who was up sixty thousand dollars. He followed him into DAOs and an ENS domain claimed ahead of the airdrop. “That airdrop kind of was like my green light, like I’m here to play.”

The podcast came first, though. He had been planning one for months under a deliberately provocative name, wanting to “somehow create value for Black people and Black culture and just like have more conversations around this like super provocative language”. He puts that down to leaving Black high school spaces for a mostly white campus, where “I almost found myself censoring myself”.

Then he went looking for something to read about crypto, and found Ponzi schemes. “I definitely didn’t really see any Black people making content that was informative.” He raps, so he chased music and crypto instead of a job. Eighty dollars bought a year in a music and tech research DAO, where he asked on day one how to get paid. They put him on interviews with people collecting music NFTs, a market already dying. “So I was immediately put right in the middle of the meta.”

The bigger idea he pitched in person at FWB Fest: “how can a DAO be the new music label?” The argument is about working conditions, not tokens. What he got was funding for a podcast, and he ran with it rather than reassess, because “I felt a little bit like scared I was going to lose my funding if I was a little too honest about how overwhelmed I was”. Then the conferences: ETH Denver, ETHCC, DevCon in Thailand, a couple of months in Nigeria, a month in Kenya.

The middle of the tape is not about crypto. He studied psychology and philosophy, and what stuck was perception. He lists his own vantage point, a straight Black man from a low income background, most of his life spent in white rooms. “I speak the way I speak, which affords me a certain level of privilege.” The question he carries into interviews now: “what are the modes of expression people I’m meeting leave behind” to get through the room. One host answers it himself, on being raised Zoroastrian and on a month in India in sixth grade.

Asked how he is prepping for the next bull run, he sells nothing. “I feel like I’m in the iteration cycle at this point.” Event coverage was tried and did not stick, the media work rests on one person, and he says why: “I feel like I haven’t allocated capital in a way that’s left him feeling supported and left him feeling stressed and overworked”. The goal has not moved, “we want to see Africans and people of African descent become first adopters of blockchain technology”, though he counts two of the continent’s fifty four countries: “clearly I’m not the one representing the diaspora”. An entrepreneur summit left him one finding: “I got really aware my business acumen is not very big, like it’s negligible actually.”

The lightning round is where he plants a flag. Blockchain or AI? “I use AI every day, but I feel like without blockchain, I would never have started to ask the questions I’ve been asking about money.” Then the reason. “I think blockchain is, in my mind, what makes the idea of money truly being a public good possible for the first time in human history.” AI, he reckons, will probably take the labor economy with it. He picks movies, owns up to an obscene amount of anime, and jumps early for another call.
