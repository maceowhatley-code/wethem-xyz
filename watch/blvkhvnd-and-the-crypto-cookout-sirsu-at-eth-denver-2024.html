BLVKHVND and the Crypto Cookout: Sirsu.

Sirsu, Reported by Maceo, Denver, 30 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

To quote this piece: Sirsu, “BLVKHVND and the Crypto Cookout: Sirsu,” We Them Media, https://wethem.xyz/watch/blvkhvnd-and-the-crypto-cookout-sirsu-at-eth-denver-2024. Each transcript line links to its second.

ETH Denver 2024, filmed in the back room of a party because that is where they were, and it is one of the pieces FWB paid for: “Shout out to FWB for giving me money.” Across from Maceo is Sirsu, who was, as Maceo puts it, “the first person I met in this space at all” after graduation.

The first half is how he got here, and it turns on one friend and one regret. A designer he knew was selling Instagram filters as NFTs; Sirsu saw the bids and got in. The reason he listened is a story about an earlier chance he did not take, art on a Kanye CD, “he was like, bro, put your art on here. You’re going to get dumb exposure. I dubbed it because I was too busy doing other shit.” His friend went on to work with Virgil. “So the next time he told me about the NFT shit, I was like, alright, okay, you know what you’re talking about.” The lesson he draws is about who to trust: “there are certain people you meet in your life that like have that kind of conviction and clarity.”

Then he paid it forward, and the method is the best thing in the piece. He would find an artist he liked, ask them to mint, ask them what they thought it was worth, and buy it himself. “The best way I can show you that this shit works is if you mint it and somebody buys it.” When they offered the money back: “No, keep it, the fuck? Like, now do it to somebody else.”

BLVKHVND, in 2021, came out of Twitch. He and his friends were streaming through the pandemic and helping smaller communities with overlays and music, and then, after Juneteenth 2020, a lot of Black and brown streamers stopped streaming in protest, “even though like streamings are livelihood.” That made him ask “how can we distribute value to people?”, and the answer was to take what Mint Fund had done for artists and bring it to gaming: an esports organisation “that’s owned by everyone else.” The scale it reached: “We got 450 or so people mostly new to crypto largely Black and brown folks to buy two crypto punks. We raised about one and a half million dollars at the time.”

And then the part that is not about money at all. Those auctions closed while he was in a hospital with his father, who died during them. “I honestly like at that point in time it was weird because it was like my best advocate he was in a coma for a whole month. I couldn’t even tell him what was happening.” He describes being “both incredibly sad as shit and also elated.”

The last five minutes are advice, and it is unusually responsible for the genre. If you are curious, go to an event, because “presence is more important than anything else.” If you are broke, mint online where it is free. And if the market is running: “if you’re here And you know you got bills to pay And you got tokens you done claimed, sell that shit right now,” because “real life is way more important than fun money.” He has watched people refuse: “I know too many people that were up one day, 20, 30, 40, 50, 100,000 dollars,” who did not sell. Get an accountant. “Handle your business.”

They close on where they started, a bookshop in Philadelphia: “Uncle Bobby says Uncle Bobby, shouts to Mark And the whole team of Uncle Bobby’s,” and what they want next, “to work together to bring Philadelphia on chain.” Language is unfiltered; the show intro uses its earlier name.
