The self-proclaimed biggest bandit in Denver.

Jonathan Griffin, Reported by Maceo, Denver, 6 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

A man four months out of a ten-year federal sentence walks his old corner in Denver and explains what changed while he was gone. He introduces himself by the name the papers gave him and asks you to look it up.

“I’m known as the Clearinghouse Bandit here in Denver. You can look me up online.” He says he robbed fourteen banks in the city and did ten years for it. He is from St. Louis, and this corner was the first place he hung out when he arrived.

What he has done since he got out is stated as plainly as what he did before: “I got a job. I’m working UPS. Basically staying out of trouble. Been drug-free for like ten years. I don’t use. I only smoke cigarettes.” He is passing through on the way to a friend’s house, looking at old memories.

His account of Pearl and Colfax is local history from somebody who was part of it. “Pearl and Colfax used to be one of the most money-getting streets in this city. You could stand on Pearl and Colfax and make a thousand dollars an hour.” Now: “There ain’t no hustlers down here any more.”

The most useful thing in the piece is his economics of the neighbourhood, which lands on the same conclusion as the housing advocates without any of their vocabulary. “When marijuana came in, it drove up the cost of living here. These motels used to be like thirty-five, forty dollars a night. Now they’re like seventy-five, eighty. You could rent one for a week for three hundred. Now you can’t. That’s why you got so many homeless people out here.” He never lived on the street himself; he always kept a motel room. He says that option no longer exists at that price for anybody.

Asked about the internet, he answers with a law of nature: “Anything that starts off good is going to end up bad. Anything that starts off bad can turn good.” But he thinks the internet is, on balance, good, because people learn faster on it.

And a closing observation from someone with a professional view of the subject: what put a dent in crime, in his experience across the country, was not DNA. It was mobile phones, because anybody can call the police to the scene and take a picture.
