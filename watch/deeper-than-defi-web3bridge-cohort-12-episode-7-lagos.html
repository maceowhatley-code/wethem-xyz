A developer on what smart contracts are for.

Joseph, Lagos, 8 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

Executive producer: C.Y. Lee

Seven minutes in Ikorodu, Lagos at the end of Web3Bridge Cohort 12, the seventh episode in a run of short interviews We Them Media filmed with the developers finishing the course.

Web3Bridge founder Ayodeji Awosika opens from the same building, counting the cohort in and out and listing where applications for the next one came from.

The subject introduces himself briefly: “my name is Joseph and I’m from Anambra State and I’m Igbo by tribe”. He was already a developer, JavaScript and Python, and heard about Web3 through “an ex-classmate, Oluchi by name” who had been through an earlier cohort.

Asked for a favourite memory he will not pick one. Asked to describe the course to somebody else, he does not sell it. It is “mental draining”, he says, and “it will get you to think outside the box”. His warning is short: “you have to like be mentally prepared”.

On community he starts small and moves out. “the community is vast”, but “you start from somewhere”, and the somewhere is the classroom: “It starts actually from here, from the classmates and all that, meeting a lot of people.” After that, “Web3 Bridge gave us the opportunity to go for events”, and the network grows from there.

His reflection on the cohort is that it is a beginning rather than an end. “first of all, there’s a lot more to learn, so this is not just where it stops.” His team is building RaceTree, “an app, a dApp that connects investors to founders easily”. The problem he names is on the funding side: investors “fund the startups and yeah, there’s nothing to show for it. There’s no gauge.” The fix is mechanical. “So funds are being released based on milestones.”

What excites him about smart contracts is how far past the obvious they go. “We feel it’s just based on money, the DeFi aspect and all that. But then you get to go deeper and deeper and you begin to see things like NFTs, DeSci, and the rest and the likes.” His own pull is elsewhere: “The side of crypto that interests me the most is the gaming aspect”, though the cohort’s work has been on DeFi and on contracts that hold up.

The last question is how much Nigeria actually knows about crypto. He thinks it is slow work. “it’s going to take a lot of time. It’s not something that will like happen ones”, because the vocabulary “sounds new to the layman”. What he wants is education that leaves the top layer and goes “to the markets, like penetrate and actually like teach people to understand this thing”, so the whole field is not mistaken for tap-to-earn.
