Gentrification and Black Pride, on the 23 bus.

Ranier, Reported by Maceo, Philadelphia, 10 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

Answers How has the place you live changed in your lifetime? and What would you say to someone under ten?, see how other cities answered

On the 23 bus in Philadelphia, Ranier, 33, from North Philadelphia, is on his way to R-Fest, the Black Pride festival, when Maceo asks what he thinks of the city. “It’s gentrifying. It’s beautiful though. Like I love my city.” The cost is personal. “The negative impact is that all of the kids I grew up with is now gone. I had the liberty of owning my house. I paid taxes like my mama taught me and I still got my mama house.”

A cosmetologist since 21 who says he does New York Fashion Week twice a year, he explains what R-Fest is for. “Every June is Pride. So R-Fest is just, you know, it acknowledges the Black people a little bit more. It highlights the different people of color that’s around the city that’s doing big things for the Pride community.” The block party is on 13th and Walnut in front of Woody’s, and it is free: “We love free things, right? Amen.” He shouts out the Phirm, “like a black gay community” that runs events across the city, “because it’s not a lot of them.”

Asked what a reporter should cover in Philadelphia this summer, he points to the people around him. “People are going through a lot of issues here in Philadelphia and it’s not too many people trying to help. It’s a lot of resources, but people don’t know how to open up their phone and look up these different programs.” Then a message for children under ten: “Never stop, keep dreaming, believe in yourself, pat yourself on the back when you do good accomplishments and listen to your mama.” And for his own mother: “She taught me how to be exactly who I am.”
