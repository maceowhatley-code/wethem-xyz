Twenty years in a room somebody wants to sell.

Chiquis Ayala, Denver, 13 min, filmed 3 April 2026

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week

Twelve minutes inside a Denver theater on a First Friday, filmed between sets with the house band still on the stand. It begins as a lineup introduction and turns into a pitch, because the building is for sale.

The band introduces itself first: “we just get together the first Friday of the month, so we all play with different bands”. The lineup gets read out over the room, Chiquis Ayala on the bass, “We got Dave Miller on the drums”, and a harmonica player whose instrument does not look like a harmonica. “Yeah, it’s like a 1910 amplifier”, he says, “walnut wood and the cornet”. He built it out of a practical problem: “I was playing at the Telluride Bluegrass Festival and they don’t want you to amplify”, which meant “playing over 30 banjos and mandolins with a harmonica”. A music store had a busted cornet, and “a woodworker right around the corner, I gave him some drawings and some harmonicas”. The harps drop into the bell: “Yeah, I can change every key.”

Then the history. “Playing here, man, I’ve been playing here almost 20 years now”, he says, and the reason he stayed is a death. The man who welcomed him in “had always set a microphone up for me, and I just kind of kept it going”, and “it’s just been a whole family thing”. The owners, he says, “they’ve owned it since ’72”. “Back in the day it was all Mexican movies”, in a neighbourhood he describes the same way, and the same stage “had the Red Hot Chili Peppers play there when they were in their socks”. Now, “now it’s just Denver”.

A woman at the bar takes a turn. She is from here, but “I went to First Friday like ten years ago, and I haven’t been since”. What brought her back out: “I’m dating the drummer.”

The last third belongs to the bass player. “I’m Chiquis Ayala, and I’ve been here in Denver for about 40 years”, he says, and “I’ve been performing here in Astlan Bar for about 20 years”. He frames the sale as a fork: “nowadays the developers want just a place. Just a lot to build apartments”, where the owners “want somebody to buy it and to keep it as a historical place”. His argument is that “this place is a jewel”, grounded in the room’s own record: la causa, organisers who met here when “back in the day Hispanics, they’re not allowed in universities or nothing like that”. The price, as he heard it, is “probably about three million. But again, I don’t know”. He tells it again in Spanish, then says he is a blues bassist who plays Latin jazz and flamenco, brought up from Juarez, Chihuahua to play. The ask comes last, and plainly: “Well, the theater’s for sale, and anybody wants to buy it, have them call me.” Nobody on the tape sounds confident it will work.
