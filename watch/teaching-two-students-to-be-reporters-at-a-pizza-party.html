Benny and Estabon, Temple musicians turned street reporters for the day.

Reported by Maceo · Philadelphia · 24 min

In this video? Ask for a change or have it taken down.

Keep up with new releases. Get the newsletter, once a week →

Two Temple students who make music and know nothing about crypto get handed a microphone at a Philadelphia pizza party and are taught to conduct interviews in real time. Twenty-four minutes, and it is the closest thing on this channel to watching the method get built.

Benny and Estabon introduce themselves as multifaceted: music, production, strings, video, audio, events. Then they are asked what they want out of the night, and Benny produces the best summary of Pizza DAO anyone gives all evening: what a stranger should take away is “that DAOs can exist for reasons other than to rug people.”

Which requires somebody to explain what one is, so it gets explained on camera in plain language: “A DAO is any organisation that governs a blockchain based treasury through voting mechanisms.” Then concretely, for this one: a staked position in ETH earning interest, other assets, and a governance process for spending it on pizza that is open to holders of the pizza box NFTs. The analogy offered is a shareholder meeting, with tokens instead of certificates. On where the money comes from: sponsors now, roughly ten of them, though the original raise was an NFT sale, “which was the thing when people cared.”

Halfway through, the reason this piece matters to the rest of the site. Asked what he is doing next, Maceo describes the plan out loud: “I’m trying to see, can we set up different kinds of pseudo chapters of content creators who leverage our platform.” Friends in Nigeria were already on board. Friends in Kenya were “iffy”. Both of those are now projects on this site with their own pages, so this is the founding statement, made at a party, before either existed.

The rest is two beginners learning by doing, and it is funnier and more instructive than a tidier version would be. A lore breakdown on a local figure with a fast scooter. A digression about being born metric. And an accidental lesson in sourcing when a claim gets tested by asking a man at the bar whether it is a known fact, he says yes, and somebody immediately names it: bad journalism.

There is also a real note on craft buried in it. Offered a pair of glasses to wear on camera, he refuses: “I feel like I’m falling into an archetype of people who interview people with glasses on.”

It closes on a stranger the students interview themselves, who makes the case for why young people should hold crypto and then makes a much better case about risk: lose thirty per cent of ten thousand at thirty-two, or thirty per cent of a much larger number at fifty-seven, and ask which one actually hurts.
