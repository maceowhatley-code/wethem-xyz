It is ironic and historically significant that the Voting Rights Act was signed on August 6th, 1965, and that President Richard Nixon suspended the dollar's international convertibility into gold almost exactly 6 years later on August 15th, 1971. At that point, African Americans had barely begun exercising federally protected electoral power on a broader scale in the nation, entered a new monetary era, one in which the value of money would increasingly depend on government policy rather than on convertibility into a scarce monetary asset. This chronology does not establish that the 2 events were causally connected. It does, however, reveal a consequential convergence. Black Americans gained greater access to the political system, just as the economic rules governing wages, savings, credit, debt, and asset ownership were being fundamentally altered. The ballot gave African Americans a mere meaningful voice in selecting those who governed. It did not give them comparable influence over the monetary institutions that determined whether their wages retained value, whether their savings accumulated purchasing power, or whether their children could acquire appreciating assets. Political inclusion had expanded, but monetary power had remained concentrated elsewhere.
