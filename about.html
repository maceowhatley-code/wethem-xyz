Mission

What would you ask, if you could ask the world?

Almost everything you know about the rest of the world reached you through a system you cannot see into. We Them Media runs the other way. A camera in the hands of someone who lives there, asking the question, keeping the answer in their own voice.

The short version

We Them Media, in five lines.

What it is. A community-driven documentary journalism platform: ordinary people, filmed on their own streets, talking about how money and technology actually reach them.

Who and when. Founded in 2021 by Maceo Whatley, with contributing reporters filing from where they live. The team is on the Team page.

How it is made. Every piece is free to watch. Quotes are verbatim from the tape, every transcript links each line to the second it was said, and speakers are named as they gave their names.

How to quote us. Name the speaker, the piece and We Them Media, and link the watch page. Each watch page prints the line to copy.

How to take part. Watch in the library, back the work on the support page, hire us through Work with us, or read The We Them Weekly.

What is missing

Why this exists. In the founder’s words.

Before now I never envisioned myself running a newsroom. This started as a podcast I made to contribute something to the music industry, and that podcast is how I built the network, the community, and the archive that We Them Media is now built on. What I learned in web3 shifted what it was for. “Originally conceived as a podcast, things have shifted-- as my journey into the realm of Web 3.0 has taught me the importance of prioritizing community over content creation.” That sentence is from September 2022 and it is still the shortest version of how We Them Media happened.

Here is the problem as I see it. Almost everything you know about a place you have never been reached you through somebody else’s edit. It came filtered, summarized, and reordered before it got to you, by people and now by machines that never asked the person it was about. Ask a question about a country you have never set foot in and watch what comes back: an answer in seconds, confidently written, from sources you cannot inspect, about people nobody talked to. You lose the ability to act on any of it, because you cannot tell what is true.

The thing I keep coming back to is that “the victors are the history books.” So this time we should do our work and archive from all sides. That is the whole idea.

What is missing is the footage. Enough video made by people who live where the story is happening that anyone can check a claim against a face. Enough of it to put communities in front of each other who would otherwise never meet. Make enough of that and people can act on what they can finally see.

The Mission
In one line

Narrative is a public good. News is a public good.

“We Them Media is a community-driven media platform trying to be at the intersection of tech, community, and culture.”

The longer version is the one I wrote in 2024 and still stand behind: our mission is “to communicate the viability of blockchain technology as a tool to expand the agency of Black communities to have a new level of control over our economies, culture, and ideas.” The way we do it now is street journalism. The idea is, all of a sudden you’ve got a neighbor committed to being your neighborly newscaster. Businesses shutting down, people getting evicted, good deeds, accomplishments, tragedy. All of it. We put up a question every week, people around the world go out and ask it on their own streets, and the answers go up with the person’s name and city on them. Narrative is a public good. News is a public good. I want the people the rest of the industry categorizes, generalizes, or ignores to be the ones holding the camera.

The Community
Who we serve

The “Them” in our name is the people who get spoken about rather than spoken with.

We make videos from and for communities whose stories are most often told extractively, by outsiders passing through a place they don’t share, or not told at all.

There’s a pattern behind that extraction. It’s colonial, neo-colonial, and corporate, and we don’t pretend it isn’t. Colonialism in particular is the biggest unwritten story of contemporary media. We treat it as a lens through which other stories get told. George Floyd needed colonial context to be told justly. Biafra needs it. Land and economic questions in Kenya carry the long shadow of British settler structures, even when the day’s news looks unrelated. Eviction in Charlotte needs colonial-and-corporate context. Most outlets leave that context out. That’s how communities end up dehumanized and decontextualized in the same breath. We don’t.

The work is led from Africa and the diaspora. White collaborators and other allied voices join the network on the same anti-extractive terms. Where you come from matters here. It is not a test you pass or fail. The question we ask anyone joining us is whether they treat the people in their videos as partners or as objects.

The network is anchored in Africa. That’s where most of our contributor base lives and where the founder has spent the most time on the ground. The immediate work is to show the model holds in Nigeria, Kenya and Ethiopia, then expand. We’re not committing to a second continent before the first one is properly anchored. The English-speaking diaspora is already part of the work. Expansion comes in stages.

The team is split across continent and diaspora, from Lagos and Abuja to Nairobi to Philadelphia, and everyone is named on the masthead. The audience: people who want to understand somewhere they don’t live, from someone who does.

The work is free to access. No paywall. No advertising. The audience we earn is the audience we owe.

The Stakes
Why this matters now

We’ve been pacified into complacency.

Most Americans don’t know what’s happening in the rest of the world, and when we do, we’ve been given reasons designed to make us feel there’s nothing we can do about it. So we don’t. We have been taught to feel helpless, and the feeling keeps us quiet.

The U.S. holds disproportionate weight on the planet, and most of us have been set up to undervalue that, because recognizing the power that comes with it would mean having to use it. An American citizen could push for a great deal. Most push for very little. Much of the difference comes down to not knowing what is happening, and to whom. Nobody has to hide the news when it never arrives.

The work We Them Media is committed to is closing the gap. We want the world to be able to say in 2030, “that’s how I knew what was going on.” That is a ten-year arc. Most media builds for a cycle that ends tonight. We’re working on the cycle that ends with people having context they didn’t have, and acting differently because of it.

The audience we’re filming with and the audience we’re publishing to aren’t the same audience. Africa and the diaspora are where the videos come from. The whole world is who we’re publishing to, but the U.S. specifically is where the most consequential complacency lives. So the editorial bridge is two-way on purpose.

The Model
How the media works

Video-first, conversation-led, on the ground.

WTM sits on the same shelf as independent host-led media, Lex Fridman, Channel 5, Zane Lowe, Afropolitan. The closest comparison is Channel 5, which films from an American vantage. Our contributors film from Africa, the diaspora and the U.S. We put those places in conversation with each other. We’re not in the same lane as legacy hard-news outlets and we’re not trying to be.

Everyone reports from somewhere. A newsroom that claims to stand nowhere is standing somewhere and not saying so. We say where we stand. The work is led from Africa, made with the diaspora, and filmed by people in the places they live. Put enough of those vantages side by side and you get a truer picture than any one of them alone. We don’t apologize for that. Saying where you stand is more honest than claiming you stand nowhere.

We don’t just cover communities. Coverage leaves when the story does. What we are trying to leave behind is the capacity to keep filming.

How it works: One Question is the programme. Somebody on their own street answers a question on camera before they walk away. Contributors across the network film them in the places they live. Anyone can also post a question to the platform and attach money to it, and storytellers close to that question pick it up, film it, and earn the bounty on delivery. We retain the strongest contributors at the end of each season as lead voices for their regions. Funding flows through mechanisms you can see: recurring support through Fractured Atlas, our fiscal sponsor, and open bounties on poidh where every question and every payout is public. Readers can see what they’re paying for and contributors can see what they’re being paid in. WTM holds editorial direction. Not by funders. Not by token holders. Not by partners. The conversation all of this produces is the point.

The Stack
Four layers

Four layers. Each does a different job.

None of these replace a person with a camera. Layer one is what you read. Layers two through four keep the work online, paid for and searchable if one platform disappears.

Layer 01, Editorial

We Them Media

We own the editorial direction. Partners support the rails. They don’t pick the stories.

The content happens here. Contributors shoot and write, we edit, the piece goes out under the WTM masthead. Everything else in the stack exists so this layer can do its job without depending on any one platform’s mood.

Two channels, two jobs. Paragraph hosts the long-form pieces. We sign each one onchain, so the record of who owns it sits in our wallet rather than on someone else’s server. Beehiiv sends the newsletter. The subscriber list is ours, and the mail reaches inboxes.

Status: Operating since 2021. Work from 16 countries.
Layer 02, Licensing

CTRL+X

When a piece gets republished, we know who, when, and at what rate.

Right now, when an outlet reposts a WTM piece, the only enforcement mechanism is a takedown email. CTRL+X is building rails on Solana that change that, a license attached to the piece, recorded to the chain, queryable by anyone, with terms and payments enforced at the protocol level.

Arikia Millikan, founder of CTRL+X and formerly the youngest editor at WIRED, joined WTM in May 2026 and is now Editorial Advisor.

Status: Active. Arikia Millikan joined WTM in May 2026, now Editorial Advisor.
Layer 03, Memory

bonfires.ai

You can search the notes behind each piece later. If it’s worth licensing, we license it. If it isn’t, we don’t.

Bonfires.ai sits behind the work, not in front of it. Once a piece is filmed and published, what surrounded it is kept where it can be searched later. That means the source threads, the corrections, the follow-ups, and the things we learned and didn’t use.

If WTM’s body of work makes a useful asset on Bonfires, we license access to it and the revenue flows back to the people who made the videos. If nobody’s buying, we don’t spend cycles on it.

Layer 04, Commissioning

POIDH

Bounties commission specific stories. The work is paid in stable money that crosses borders.

POIDH (pics or it didn’t happen) is the bounty platform WTM runs open contributor calls through. Anyone can claim a bounty, ship the piece, and get paid in USDC. Every fulfilled bounty mints as a moment NFT in the WTM POIDH album, the on-chain record of who filmed what, when.

Part of the POIDH platform fee on bounties WTM creates flows back to WTM on completed bounties.

Status: Live.
Operating motto

“Shots on goal.”

More content, get it out, things will work. Polish on the second pass. The first pass is just showing up. Most of media never ships because it’s waiting to be perfect first.

The Horizon
What success looks like

A self-sustaining, community-driven media platform where the people closest to a story are the ones paid to tell it.

The editorial standard is published where anyone can read it and hold us to it.

By the end of 2026: the model shown to hold in Nigeria, Kenya and Ethiopia. Three lead voices retained. The first season’s work published. Money coming in through more than one door, monthly supporters, fellowships and grants, none of them with a say over what gets filmed. By 2027 and 2028: more regions, decided by what the first ones teach us about where the model travels.

If someone asks five years from now what We Them Media changed, the answer I want is reconnection. Black America back in touch with the rest of the African diaspora. Diasporic communities back in conversation with the continent. Someone in Philadelphia watching a cousin they have never met answer a question on a street in Lagos, and recognizing the way they talk. Black Americans seeing themselves as a global people, curious about the rest of the diaspora in a new way. The videos are the means. The end is people who were split up by distance and time finding each other again.

The bigger ambition, honestly, is to create a mirror that wouldn’t exist if we weren’t doing it. A way for the world to start to see itself without the messages funded to reach us, and without the edit of people who have a stake in us not doing this. People in places that don’t even have power, getting online and on the record. That’s the work.

The people on the tape are credited by name, and so are the people who film them. Paying the people who film is what the supporter goal is for. Nobody is paid for it yet.

Standards. Named, so they can be tested.

Seven principles, applied to Maceo first
01

Rules apply to Maceo first

Every standard on this list applies to the founder before it applies to anyone else on the team. If Maceo violates a rule, it gets published as a correction, publicly.

02

A name said to the camera is the consent

A person who gives their name to the camera has agreed to be on it. That is the record. There is no signed release. Anyone in a piece can ask for a change or a takedown: message us on Instagram (@we_them_media) or X (@WeThemMediaHQ), or use the panel headed Something wrong here? at the foot of the page, and we will get back to you within two weeks.

03

We check the words against the tape

We check names, places, dates and every quoted line against the tape before a piece goes up. We do not check the claims people make: the transcripts are their words as they said them, and every transcript page says so.

04

Conflicts of interest get named

If Maceo or anyone on the team has a financial stake, a personal relationship, or a prior collaboration with a subject or funder, that gets named in the piece itself. Not disclosed later. Named up front.

05

Corrections are visible

If we get something wrong, we say so where the original ran, at the top, with the date of correction and what changed.

06

The reporting is never machine-made

What people said on camera is what they said.

07

Funding does not direct the work

Supporters fund the work. They do not choose stories, edit copy, or veto pieces. Editorial direction sits with WTM. The bills are published so anyone can audit that the money and the work stay separate.

These seven make the standard visible. If any principle here is broken, the mechanism to name it is a DM to any of our social pages or a reply to The We Them Weekly. Corrections get published where the original ran.

All of a sudden you’ve got a neighbor committed to being your neighborly newscaster.
by Maceo, founder, We Them Media
